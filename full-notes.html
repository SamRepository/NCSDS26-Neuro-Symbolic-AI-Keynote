Speaker notes — Neuro-Symbolic AI

How knowledge graphs and ontologies are learning to live inside large language models — and why that changes everything.
Dr. Samir Sellami · NCSDS-26 · ENSET-Skikda · Target 20:00
Arc. History (slides 1–8) → Limits of LLMs (9–10) → Structure returns: KGs & ontologies (11–13) → Synthesis: KiL + evidence + cases (14–20) → Close (21).   Two key transitions: slide 8 (Heinlein — the turning point) and slide 14 (KiL — the architecture). The whole talk pivots on these two.
01 Title — Welcome & self-intro 0:00 · ~75s
Slide shows: title, your name, affiliation, graph motif, NCSDS & ENSET logos.
Good morning all, and thanks to the organizers at ENSET-Skikda. I am Samir SELLAMI, researcher at LIRE Laboratory, Univ-Constantine 2, and Associate teacher at the Department of Mathematics & Computer Science, ENSET-Skikda. Over the next twenty minutes I want to share a short journey. How we got to large language models, LLMs. Why they alone are not enough. And why the next decade belongs to a merge of neural networks and symbolic knowledge. Let's begin.
02 Agenda — Four short acts 1:15 · ~45s
Slide shows: numbered outline of the four acts.
Four short acts for the agenda of today's talk. First, where we came from — symbolic and neural. Second, the honest limits of today's LLMs. Third, the quiet return of knowledge graphs and ontologies. Fourth, the synthesis — Knowledge-Infused Learning — and what it unlocks. Finally, a simple call to action.
PART 01 History — two traditions, one goal Slides 3–8 · ~5 min
03 Two traditions, one goal 2:00 · ~45s
Slide shows: section header on accent background — "One goal, two tribes."
AI has always had two main directions. Symbolists — intelligence as logic, rules and ontologies. Connectionists — intelligence as patterns learned from data. For seventy years they barely talked. Today, for the first time, they have to collaborate. That is the story of this talk.
04 The symbolic era — 1956 to 2000 2:45 · ~60s
Slide shows: timeline — Dartmouth · Expert systems · OWL/Semantic Web · The winter.
From the nineteen-fifties to two thousand, symbolic AI ruled. Expert systems. Ontologies. The Semantic Web vision. Beautiful and transparent — you could read the rules. But brittle and too hard. Every edge case had to be coded by hand, and the world had too many edges and entities. By the late nineties, funding collapsed and we entered the AI winter big freeze. A beautiful theory frozen — and it was time for data and deep learning.
05 The neural renaissance — 2012 to 2022 3:45 · ~60s
Slide shows: dark frame, ImageNet error-rate sparkline (28% → 2.3%), timeline cells.
Twenty-twelve everything changed — ImageNet, deep CNNs, machines could see. Twenty-seventeen — Attention is all you need, the Transformer arrives. Twenty-twenty — GPT-3 and scaling laws. November twenty-twenty-two — ChatGPT. AI left the lab. What symbolic AI could not do — see, hear, translate, converse — the neural tradition did, decisively.
06 LLMs — a methodological revolution 4:45 · ~45s
Slide shows: pull-quote — "A single prompt now does the work of an entire NLP pipeline."
In fact, LLMs started a methodological revolution we are living through. What used to take five tools and a week — tokenisation, parsing, entity extraction, the whole NLP pipeline — now takes one prompt and one second. Raw text in, structured meaning out. For a doctor, a lawyer, a researcher, this is extraordinarily empowering.
07 What deep learning got right 5:30 · ~45s
Slide shows: three columns — Perception · Language · Science & autonomy.
Let me be fair. Deep learning and transformers did things symbolic AI never could. Image and speech recognition. Machine translation. Protein folding. Autonomous driving. Superhuman game-play. The neural camp won the last decade, decisively. I am not here to argue against that. I am here to argue about what comes next.
08 No free lunch — the turning point 6:15 · ~45s
Slide shows: dark frame, large quote — "There ain't no such thing as a free lunch." — Heinlein, 1966.
But — there ain't no such thing as a free lunch. Robert Heinlein said it sixty years ago, and it fits AI perfectly. Every gain in convenience has a hidden cost. The cost of the LLM revolution is now starting to show. Let's be honest about what it is.

PART 02 The limits — where LLMs fail Slides 9–10 · ~1.5 min
09 The LLM trust crisis 7:00 · ~50s
Slide shows: five failure modes — hallucination · no provenance · no consistency · hard to update · opaque.
LLMs are fluent and confident. But often disconnected from reality. They hallucinate — wrong answers that sound perfect. No provenance. No consistency. Hard to update. GPU and tokens hungry. Their knowledge is entangled in billions of weights. In medicine, law, finance — fluent confidence without grounding is not a feature. It is a liability.
10 Statistics is not knowledge 7:50 · ~45s
Slide shows: two-column diagram — implicit (LLM weights, black box) vs explicit (KG, glass box).
Here is the deeper problem. An LLM learns what words follow what words. It does not really know what a patient, a contract, or a regulation is. The knowledge is implicit — entangled in weights, impossible to inspect. A knowledge graph is the opposite. Explicit. Queryable. Auditable. A glass box, not a black box.
PART 03 Structure — the quiet return of meaning Slides 11–13 · ~2.75 min
11 Knowledge graphs, revisited 8:35 · ~75s
Slide shows: accent frame, live KG diagram — Patient · Ibuprofen · NSAID · Medication · Arthritis · Inflammation · Warfarin · Thrombosis.
This is what a knowledge graph looks like. Eight nodes, each represents a real thing. For example, Medication, Patient, Ibuprofen, Arthritis, Inflammation. The lines between them are typed relationships — not just lines, but meaning. Patient takes Ibuprofen, which is-a Nonsteroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drug. Ibuprofen treats Arthritis, which has Inflammation as a symptom. Ibuprofen interacts with Warfarin, which treats Thrombosis. Now imagine this at the scale of a hospital, or all of medicine. Every fact written down, inspectable, updatable. That is what an LLM alone cannot give you.
12 Ontologies — the shape of meaning 9:50 · ~45s
Slide shows: KG vs OWL vs SHACL — three rows with examples.
And above the graph sits the ontology. The ontology is the shape of meaning. For example, a patient has exactly one date of birth. A contract has at least two parties. These are not statistical tendencies — they are truths of the domain. Remember this line: the graph is the memory, the ontology is the grammar.
13 Ontology is having its moment 10:35 · ~45s
Slide shows: dark frame, large quote attributed to Tony Seale, 2025.
Tony Seale recently wrote on LinkedIn that ontology is having its moment. He is right. For twenty years ontologies were a quiet corner of computer science. Now every major enterprise is building one. Because to deploy AI at scale, inside a regulated business, you need a layer of ground truth the model can consult.
PART 04 The synthesis — Knowledge-Infused Learning Slides 14–20 · ~7 min
14 Knowledge-Infused Learning — the architecture 11:20 · ~75s
Slide shows: KiL loop diagram — Query → LLM ↔ KG → Verifier → Output, with feedback arc.
Look at this diagram — the architecture of the next decade of AI pipelines. A user asks a question. The LLM does not answer from memory alone. It queries the knowledge graph. The graph returns grounded facts. The LLM drafts an answer. Then a symbolic check — does it satisfy the ontology? Only then does it reach the user and give an output, with provenance attached. And the dashed loop at the bottom — every interaction refines the graph. Neural intuition, times symbolic truth. More accurate. More explainable. More efficient. This is Knowledge-Infused Learning.
⚑ Densest slide. Don't speed up. Point to the feedback arrow at the bottom — that's the line that makes it stick.
15 The evidence — numbers behind the claim 12:35 · ~60s
Slide shows: four cells with +71% · +77.6% · −40% · Substantial — Soman, Xu, MEGA-RAG, Edge.
And the benchmarks now back this up. SPOKE plus Llama-2 — a 71% boost on biomedical multiple-choice QA. LinkedIn's customer-service knowledge graph cut median resolution time by 28.6%. MEGA-RAG, in public health, reduced hallucinations by over 40%. And Microsoft's GraphRAG showed substantial gains on million-token corpora. Different domains. Different metrics. Same direction. Structure does not replace the model. It makes the model accountable.
⚑ The four numbers must land. Practice these aloud. Hit each one with a slight pause before the percentage.
16 Three ways to inject knowledge 13:35 · ~45s
Slide shows: pipeline diagram — ① Before (pre-training) · ② During (RAG) · ③ After (Verifier).
There are three points where you can inject knowledge. Before the model — pre-training phase on structured data. During inference — this is RAG, and Graph-RAG does it even better. Or after — a symbolic verifier checks the output against the ontology. Real systems combine all three. The architecture is a pipeline, not a swap.
17 In practice — healthcare 14:20 · ~45s
Slide shows: case 01 — stethoscope icon, Without KG / With KG / Result.
IN PRACTICE — Healthcare, for example. A clinician asks about a drug interaction. Without grounding, the model invents imaginary facts. With ontologies such as UMLS or SNOMED behind it, every claim is traceable. The ontology catches contradictions before they reach the patient. That is the difference between a toy and a tool. Regulators are now asking for this explicitly.
18 In practice — legal & compliance 15:05 · ~45s
Slide shows: case 02 — dark frame, scales icon.
Another use case is Legal and compliance. The goal is to review a thousand contracts without missing a clause. An LLM alone misses obligations and contradicts itself. With a legal ontology — parties, obligations, jurisdictions, dates — cross-document reasoning becomes possible. For example, European regulators are pushing this direction hard.
19 In practice — enterprise knowledge 15:50 · ~45s
Slide shows: case 03 — building icon.
In Enterprises. Every large company has a decade of documents, databases, wikis, all siloed. Build a knowledge graph over it. Connect the LLM as a natural-language interface. Now you have an assistant that actually knows your business — grounded in your own truth, not a general model guessing. A competitive advantage that no competitor can simply download.
20 In practice — education 16:35 · ~75s
Slide shows: case 04 — mortarboard icon, three numbered tracks.
And finally education — which matters especially to us here in Algeria. How do we train the next generation when LLMs make the basics seem optional? My answer is concrete. Three tracks every CS programme can add now. One — ontology engineering: OWL and SHACL. Two — knowledge-graph systems: SPARQL, Cypher, Graph-RAG. Three — a grounded-AI capstone: build a KG-backed, SHACL-validated RAG system. The neural layer will commoditise. The knowledge layer is where expertise lives — and where Algerian research can lead.

21 The outlook & a call to action 17:50 · ~45s
Slide shows: dark frame, large display type — "Build with both hands."
Here is what I want you to take away. Generative AI is powerful, accessible, empowering — and it needs control. Knowledge graphs and symbolic reasoning provide that grounding. The future of AI is not neural. It is not symbolic. It is both. Build with both hands. Thank you very much.
