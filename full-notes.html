Speaker notes — Neuro-Symbolic AI

How knowledge graphs and ontologies are learning to live inside large language models — and why that changes everything.

PART 01 History — two traditions, one goal Slides 3–9 · ~6 min
03 Two traditions, one goal 2:00 · ~45s
Slide shows: section header on accent background — "One goal, two tribes."
AI has always had two main directions. Symbolists — intelligence as logic, rules and ontologies. Connectionists — intelligence as patterns learned from data. For seventy years they barely talked. Today, for the first time, they have to collaborate. That is the story of this talk.

05 The symbolic era — 1956 to 2000 4:00 · ~60s
Slide shows: timeline — Dartmouth · Expert systems · OWL/Semantic Web · The winter.
From the nineteen-fifties to two thousand, symbolic AI ruled. Expert systems. Ontologies. The Semantic Web vision. Beautiful and transparent — you could read the rules. But brittle and too hard. Every edge case had to be coded by hand, and the world had too many edges and entities. By the late nineties, funding collapsed and we entered the AI winter — the big freeze. A beautiful theory frozen, and it was time for data and deep learning.
06 The neural renaissance — 2012 to 2022 5:00 · ~60s
Slide shows: dark frame, ImageNet error-rate sparkline (28% → 2.3%), timeline cells.
Twenty-twelve everything changed — ImageNet, deep CNNs, machines could see. Twenty-seventeen — Attention is all you need, the Transformer arrives. Twenty-twenty — GPT-3 and scaling laws. November twenty-twenty-two — ChatGPT. AI left the lab. What symbolic AI could not do — see, hear, translate, converse — the neural tradition did, decisively.
07 LLMs — a methodological revolution 6:00 · ~60s
Slide shows: pull-quote + before/after pipeline depiction — faded 8-tile classical pipeline above (≈ 5 tools · ~1 week pill), single-LLM call below (≈ 1 prompt · ~1 second pill).
In fact, LLMs started a methodological revolution we are living through. What used to take five tools and a week — tokenisation, parsing, entity extraction, the whole NLP pipeline — now takes one prompt and one second. Look at the depiction at the bottom: before, a long faded chain of stages; now, a single LLM call producing structured JSON. Raw text in, structured meaning out. For a doctor, a lawyer, a researcher, this is extraordinarily empowering.
08 What deep learning got right 7:00 · ~45s
Slide shows: three columns with icons — eye (Perception) · speech bubble (Language) · atom (Science & autonomy).
Let me be fair. Deep learning and transformers did things symbolic AI never could. Perception — image and speech recognition. Language — machine translation, TTS, fluent conversation. Science & autonomy — protein folding, autonomous driving, superhuman game-play. The neural camp won the last decade, decisively. I am not here to argue against that. I am here to argue about what comes next.

But — there ain't no such thing as a free lunch. Robert Heinlein said it sixty years ago, and it fits AI perfectly. Every gain in convenience has a hidden cost. The cost of the LLM revolution is now starting to show. Let's be honest about what it is.

PART 02 The limits — where LLMs fail Slides 10–11 · ~2 min
10 The LLM trust crisis 8:30 · ~60s
Slide shows: indented list of six critiques, each with its own icon — ⚠ hallucination · ❓ no provenance · ⑀ no consistency · 🔒 hard to update · ▦ GPU/tokens hungry · 👁/ opaque.
LLMs are fluent and confident. But often disconnected from reality. They hallucinate — wrong answers that sound perfect. No provenance — can't tell you where a fact came from. No consistency — same question, contradictory answers. Hard to update — new facts need retraining, not editing. GPU and tokens hungry. Opaque — knowledge entangled in billions of weights. In medicine, law, finance — fluent confidence without grounding is not a feature. It is a liability.
11 Statistics is not knowledge 9:30 · ~60s
Slide shows: two-column diagram — implicit (LLM weights, black box, left) vs explicit (KG, glass box, right). Analogy strip at the bottom.
Here is the deeper problem. An LLM learns what words follow what words. It does not, in any strong sense, know what a patient, a contract, or a regulation is. The knowledge is implicit — entangled in weights, impossible to inspect. A knowledge graph is the opposite. Explicit. Queryable. Auditable. A glass box, not a black box. 💡 Analogy: the LLM (left) is the engine of the car — powerful, but driving like a teenager by instinct; the knowledge graph (right) is the steering wheel — a city-map expert giving direction.

PART 03 Structure — the quiet return of meaning Slides 12–14 · ~2.75 min
12 Knowledge graphs, revisited 10:30 · ~75s
Slide shows: accent frame, live KG diagram — Patient · Ibuprofen · NSAID · Medication · Inflammation · Arthritis · Warfarin · Thrombosis.
This is what a knowledge graph looks like. Eight nodes, each represents a real thing. For example: Medication, Patient, Ibuprofen, Arthritis, Inflammation. The lines between them are typed relationships — not just lines, but meaning. Patient takes Ibuprofen, which is-a Nonsteroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drug. Ibuprofen treats Arthritis, which has Inflammation as a symptom. Ibuprofen interacts with Warfarin, which treats Thrombosis. Now imagine this at the scale of a hospital, or all of medicine. Every fact written down, inspectable, updatable. That is what an LLM alone cannot give you.
13 Ontologies — the shape of meaning 11:45 · ~45s
Slide shows: KG vs OWL vs SHACL — three rows with examples.
And above the graph sits the ontology. The ontology is the shape of meaning. For example, a patient has exactly one date of birth. A contract has at least two parties. These are not statistical tendencies — they are truths of the domain. Remember this line: the graph is the memory, the ontology is the grammar.
14 Ontology is having its moment 12:30 · ~45s
Slide shows: dark frame, large quote attributed to Tony Seale, 2025 (LinkedIn).
Tony Seale recently wrote on LinkedIn that ontology is having its moment. He is right. For twenty years ontologies were a quiet corner of computer science. Now every major enterprise is building one. Because to deploy AI at scale, inside a regulated business, you need a layer of ground truth the model can consult.
PART 04 The synthesis — Knowledge-Infused Learning Slides 15–21 · ~7.5 min
15 Knowledge-Infused Learning — the architecture 13:15 · ~90s
Slide shows: KiL loop diagram (Query → LLM ↔ KG → Verifier → Output → TO USER, all top-row aligned) with dashed feedback loop through human-in-the-loop icon back to KG (left side). Analogy strip at the bottom.
Look at this diagram — the architecture of the next decade of AI pipelines. A user asks a question. The LLM does not answer from memory alone. It queries the knowledge graph — retrieve and ground. The graph returns grounded facts. The LLM drafts an answer. Then a symbolic check — does it satisfy the ontology, SHACL, the rules? Only then does it exit to the user as a grounded answer with provenance attached. And the dashed loop at the bottom — every interaction refines the graph, with a human in the loop to curate. Neural intuition, times symbolic truth. More accurate. More explainable. More efficient. This is Knowledge-Infused Learning. 💡 Analogy: the LLM is the smooth, charismatic salesperson — promising a deal to the CEO; the knowledge graph & ontology are the strict back-room accountant — auditing every fact before the deal is signed.

16 The evidence — numbers behind the claim 14:45 · ~60s
Slide shows: four cells with +71% · +77.6% · −40% · Substantial — Soman, Xu, MEGA-RAG, Edge.
And the benchmarks now back this up. SPOKE plus Llama-2 — a 71% boost on biomedical multiple-choice QA. LinkedIn's customer-service knowledge graph cut median resolution time by 28.6%. MEGA-RAG, in public health, reduced hallucinations by over 40%. And Microsoft's GraphRAG showed substantial gains on million-token corpora. Different domains. Different metrics. Same direction. Structure does not replace the model. It makes the model accountable.

17 Three ways to inject knowledge 15:45 · ~45s
Slide shows: pipeline diagram — ① Before (pre-training) · ② During (RAG) · ③ After (Verifier).
There are three points where you can inject knowledge. Before the model — pre-training phase on structured data. During inference — this is RAG, and Graph-RAG does it even better. Or after — a symbolic verifier checks the output against the ontology. Real systems combine all three. The architecture is a pipeline, not a swap.
18 In practice — healthcare 16:30 · ~45s
Slide shows: case 01 — stethoscope icon, Without KG / With KG / Result.
IN PRACTICE — Healthcare, for example. A clinician asks about a drug interaction. Without grounding, the model invents imaginary facts. With ontologies such as UMLS or SNOMED behind it, every claim is traceable. The ontology catches contradictions before they reach the patient. That is the difference between a toy and a tool. Regulators are now asking for this explicitly.
19 In practice — legal & compliance 17:15 · ~45s
Slide shows: case 02 — dark frame, scales icon.
Another use case is Legal and compliance. The goal is to review a thousand contracts without missing a clause. An LLM alone misses obligations and contradicts itself. With a legal ontology — parties, obligations, jurisdictions, dates — cross-document reasoning becomes possible. For example, European regulators are pushing this direction hard.
20 In practice — enterprise knowledge 18:00 · ~45s
Slide shows: case 03 — building icon.
In Enterprises. Every large company has a decade of documents, databases, wikis, all siloed. Build a knowledge graph over it. Connect the LLM as a natural-language interface. Now you have an assistant that actually knows your business — grounded in your own truth, not a general model guessing. A competitive advantage no competitor can simply download.
21 In practice — education 18:45 · ~75s
Slide shows: case 04 — mortarboard icon, three numbered tracks.
And finally education — which matters especially to us here in Algeria. How do we train the next generation when LLMs make the basics seem optional? My answer is concrete. Three tracks every CS programme can add now. One — ontology engineering: OWL and SHACL. Two — knowledge-graph systems: SPARQL, Cypher, Graph-RAG. Three — a grounded-AI capstone: build a KG-backed, SHACL-validated RAG system. The neural layer will commoditise. The knowledge layer is where expertise lives — and where Algerian research can lead.
